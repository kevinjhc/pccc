01The Statue of Peace

A seated girl, and an empty chair beside her

Height
4½ ft
Depth
5 ft
Length
6 ft
Material
Bronze

The Statue of Peace is a bronze sculpture. She symbolizes the over 200,000 girls and women from 21 countries who were sexually enslaved by the Japanese Imperial Armed Forces during WWII. Commonly known as “comfort women,” this euphemistic term hid the reality of state-sanctioned and institutionalized rape. These girls and women were often tricked and forcibly kidnapped to work in “comfort stations” to serve the Japanese military.

  1. 01

    The girl

    A seated young girl with short, torn hair, bare heels, and clenched fists.

  2. 02

    The empty chair

    There is an empty chair next to her inviting the viewers to sit down and join her.

  3. 03

    The bird

    Perched on the girl’s left shoulder is a bird, symbolizing peace and the bond between the living and the dead.

  4. 04

    The shadow

    Embedded in the base as a mosaic is a figure of an older woman, the shadow of the seated girl, to represent the time that has passed and the refusal by comfort women to remain silent.

  5. 05

    The white butterfly

    Positioned over the heart of the elderly woman is a white butterfly, symbolizing the spirit of the comfort women who have already passed and hope.

The statue is sculpted by Kim Seo-Kyung and Kim Unsung.

This installation in Philadelphia will be the 18th of its kind around the world, and the 7th in the United States. The statue will be donated by the Philadelphia Peace Plaza Committee, with support from The Korean Council (정의기억연대), an organization based in Korea leading the advocacy movement on behalf of the victims of comfort women.

02History

One testimony broke the silence

In an often-silenced chapter of World War II history, over 200,000 girls and women were forced into sexual slavery by the Japanese Imperial Armed Forces from the 1930s to 1945. These girls and women were abducted, tricked, coerced, or held in debt bondage and sent to military-run or military-sanctioned brothels known as “comfort stations” and forced into daily sexual violence, abuse and torture.

One courageous testimony, however, broke the longstanding silence. On August 14, 1991, Kim Hak-soon came forward with her harrowing story, so that no one else would go through what she had experienced. Comfort women survivors worldwide have since advocated for women’s rights, peace, and justice. Their courageous and resilient efforts toward a world without war and violence against women have resonated with citizens around the world, who started grassroots efforts to build the Statue of Peace to honor the survivors’ decades-long calls for justice and remembrance.
